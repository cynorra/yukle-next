• A for-hire motor carrier's bill of lading must show at least the consignor and consignee, origin and destination points, the number of packages, a description of the freight and, where it affects the rating, its weight, volume or measurement (49 CFR 373.101).
  • The carrier's freight bill goes to the shipper or receiver that owes the charges and must show the exact rates, the total charges and the route (373.103), so whoever is named as owing the charges decides who gets the invoice.
  • A cargo claim belongs to the person entitled to recover under the bill of lading (49 U.S.C. 14706(a)), which makes the bill of lading the starting point for who may claim, and a carrier facing conflicting claims may require proof of title (49 CFR 370.3(e)).

The words shipper, carrier and consignee are used loosely, but the paperwork that follows a shipment uses them precisely, and mixing them up sends an invoice to the wrong party or a claim from someone with no right to recover. Below: who the parties are, what the federal rules put on each document, how the documents move through one shipment, and who can claim.

The Bill of Lading

Under 49 CFR 373.101, every for-hire, non-exempt motor carrier must issue a receipt or bill of lading for property tendered for interstate or foreign transportation, and must keep a record of the information under 49 CFR part 379. A failure to issue one does not affect the carrier's liability (14706(a)(1)).

The Freight Bill

The carrier also issues a freight or expense bill for each shipment (373.103). It must show: the consignor and consignee; the date of shipment; origin and destination; the number of packages; a description of the freight; weight, volume or measurement where it applies to the rating; the exact rate or rates assessed; the total charges due, including special services and where they were rendered; the route and each participating carrier; transfer points; and the address for remittance. The bill goes to the shipper or receiver owing the charges, and the carrier keeps a copy under part 379. If the bill is sent electronically by agreement, the payor gets a receipted copy on payment. Reconsigned shipments are handled differently for the names and points that appear.

The rules do not define "prepaid", "collect" or "third-party billing". Those are commercial terms. What the rule does fix is that the bill follows whoever owes the charges, so the contract or bill of lading should say who that is.

How the Documents Move: One Shipment

An invented shipment: a manufacturer in Dallas (consignor) hires a broker to move 6 pallets to a retailer in Atlanta (consignee), and the broker books a motor carrier.

  1. Tender. The pallets are tendered at Dallas. The carrier issues the bill of lading naming the manufacturer and the retailer, with 6 packages, a description and the weight.
  2. Delivery. At Atlanta two pallets are damaged. The consignee notes it on the delivery receipt. That notation is evidence, but alone it is not a claim (370.3(c)).
  3. Freight bill. The carrier issues the freight bill to the party that owes the charges, with exact rates and total charges.
  4. Claim. The person entitled to recover under the bill of lading files a written claim with the carrier identifying the shipment, asserting liability and stating an amount (370.3(b)). Deadlines and the carrier's response duties are in Freight Claims.

The broker's own records of who paid whom sit alongside this, but the broker is not the carrier and does not issue the bill of lading in a brokered move.

Who Can Claim?

QuestionAnswer from the rules
Who is liable for the loss?The receiving carrier, the delivering carrier and any other carrier over whose line the property moved under a through bill of lading (14706(a)(1))
Who may recover?The person entitled to recover under the receipt or bill of lading (14706(a)(1))
What if two parties claim the same loss?The carrier must notify all claimants and require further substantiation of each one's title or right to the claim (370.3(e))
What must the claim contain?Facts identifying the shipment, an assertion of liability and a specified or determinable amount (370.3(b))
Can liability be limited?Yes, by a reasonable value declared in writing or electronically by the shipper or agreed in writing with the carrier, except for household goods (14706(c)(1)(A))

The statute is commonly called the Carmack Amendment; that name is a popular label and does not appear in the statute's text. Packages that the carrier and shipper agree to treat as "low value" may use a streamlined documentation system in place of the usual one, and the shipper is ultimately responsible for deciding which packages qualify (373.105).

Frequently Asked Questions

Is the shipper always the owner of the goods?

No. The rules identify the shipper by role: the party that tenders the goods and may owe the charges. Ownership can sit with someone else, such as the buyer.

Can a broker issue the bill of lading?

The bill of lading rule is written for the motor carrier (373.101). A forwarder issues its own through bill of lading (373.201). A broker that only arranges transportation is neither.

Does missing paperwork end the carrier's liability?

No. Under 14706(a)(1), failing to issue a bill of lading does not affect liability.

Who receives the freight bill?

The shipper or receiver that owes the charges (373.103(a)(2)).

Scope: the federal rules for for-hire motor carriers moving property in interstate or foreign commerce. International sales terms, exempt carriers and household goods have their own rules, and none of this is legal advice.
