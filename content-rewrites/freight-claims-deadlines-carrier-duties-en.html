• A freight claim is a written communication to a proper carrier that identifies the shipment, asserts the carrier's liability and claims a specified or determinable amount of money, and a delivery-receipt notation or an appraisal report alone does not count.
  • Once a proper claim is received the carrier must acknowledge it in writing within 30 days and pay, decline or make a firm settlement offer within 120 days, with a written status update every 60 days after that if it is still pending.
  • A carrier may not give a claimant less than 9 months to file or less than 2 years to sue, and the 2 years run from the carrier's written disallowance, not from an informal refusal.

When freight is lost or damaged, two sets of clocks run: the claimant's time to file and sue, and the carrier's time to respond. They come from different sources. The claimant's minimum periods are in a federal statute, 49 U.S.C. 14706, often called the Carmack Amendment (a popular name that does not appear in the statute's text). The carrier's response duties are in the claims regulations, 49 CFR part 370. Below are the rules, a dated timeline, a calculator, a claim letter outline and the points where the bill of lading changes the answer.

Who Is Liable and When the Rules Apply

Under 14706(a)(1), a carrier providing transportation subject to the federal motor carrier statute must issue a receipt or bill of lading for property it receives. That carrier and any other carrier that delivers the property are liable to the person entitled to recover under the bill of lading, for the actual loss or injury to the property caused by the receiving carrier, the delivering carrier, or another carrier over whose line it moves under a through bill of lading. Failing to issue a bill of lading does not affect the liability. A carrier that pays may recover from the carrier responsible for the loss (14706(b)).

Part 370 governs the processing of claims for loss, damage, injury or delay to property transported in interstate or foreign commerce by motor carriers and freight forwarders (370.1). It does not set the rules for purely intrastate moves. FMCSA amended 370.1 on 19 February 2026 (91 FR 7859); the only change was to remove water carriers from the list of carriers covered, which does not affect trucking claims.

What Counts as a Claim

A claim must be a written communication from the claimant to a proper carrier, filed within the time limit in the bill of lading or contract, containing three things (370.3(b)):

  1. Facts sufficient to identify the shipment.
  2. An assertion of the carrier's liability for the loss, damage, injury or delay.
  3. A claim for the payment of a specified or determinable amount of money.

Several documents that feel like claims are not. Bad order reports, appraisal reports, notations of shortage or damage on freight bills or delivery receipts, and carrier inspection reports, standing alone, do not meet the minimum (370.3(c)). A claim for an uncertain amount, such as "$100 more or less," obliges the carrier to investigate the condition and extent of the loss, but the carrier may not voluntarily pay it until a written claim for a specified or determinable amount is filed (370.3(d)). If other carriers receive similar claims on the same shipment, the investigating carrier must notify all claimants and require further substantiation of title or right to the claim (370.3(e)).

The Claimant's Minimum Periods

Under 14706(e)(1), a carrier may not provide, by rule, contract or otherwise, a period of less than 9 months for filing a claim or less than 2 years for bringing a civil action. The 2 years run from the date the carrier gives written notice that it has disallowed part of the claim. Two rules protect claimants: an offer of compromise is not a disallowance unless the carrier says in writing that part of the claim is disallowed and gives reasons, and communications from the carrier's insurer are not a disallowance unless the insurer says so in writing, gives reasons and states that it acts for the carrier (14706(e)(2)). The statute sets minimums. A bill of lading may allow longer periods, and it also sets where the filing clock starts, which the statute's minimum does not specify. A suit may be brought in federal or State court against the delivering carrier, or against the carrier alleged to have caused the loss where the loss occurred (14706(d)).

  • 15 March 2026: the shipper sends a written claim naming the shipment, asserting liability and stating $4,200. The carrier receives it that day.
  • 14 April 2026: the carrier's written acknowledgment is due (day 30).
  • 13 July 2026: the carrier must pay, decline or make a firm offer, or send a written status (day 120).

Calculator

Rules used: 30 and 120 days from receipt, then every 60 days (Part 370); 9 months and 2 years as the shortest periods a carrier may set (14706(e)); a date that does not exist in the target month moves to that month's last day. Common mistakes: counting from the delivery date instead of the date the carrier received the claim, treating a phone call as written notice, and assuming the statutory minimum is the contract deadline.

Limiting the Carrier's Liability

14706(c)(1)(A) lets a carrier establish rates under which its liability is limited to a value set by the shipper's written or electronic declaration, or by a written agreement between carrier and shipper, if that value is reasonable under the circumstances. This is the source of "released value" terms. It excludes household goods described in 13102(10)(A), which have their own rules. So the bill of lading and any declared value matter as much as the damage itself. The statute requires the value to be "reasonable under the circumstances" and does not define the phrase.

Claim Letter Outline

A letter that meets 370.3(b) can be short. This is a format, not legal advice:

  • Heading: claimant name, carrier name, date and the words "Written claim for loss or damage."
  • Shipment: bill of lading or PRO number, ship date, origin, destination, consignee, and the items affected.
  • Liability: a sentence stating that the carrier is liable for the loss or damage to this shipment.
  • Amount: a specified dollar figure or a figure that can be determined from the attached invoice.
  • Attachments: bill of lading, freight bill, invoice or certified extract, photographs, and the delivery receipt showing the notation.
  • Delivery: keep proof of the date the carrier received it, since every response deadline runs from that date.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can the carrier pay my claim without a formal letter?

370.3(a) says a claim shall not be voluntarily paid unless it is filed as 370.3(b) describes, so an informal request can be declined, and a written claim with an amount is the safe route.

Does the 120 days mean the carrier must pay?

No. It must pay, decline or make a firm compromise offer in writing, or tell the claimant in writing why it needs longer.

When does the 2-year suit period start?

When the carrier gives written notice that it has disallowed part of the claim, per 14706(e)(1). A compromise offer alone does not start it.

Do these rules cover a broker?

Part 370 covers motor carriers and freight forwarders. A broker's liability is a separate question that this guide does not address.
