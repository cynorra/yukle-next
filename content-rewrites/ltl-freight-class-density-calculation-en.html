• Density is the weight of a handling unit as tendered divided by its cubic feet, and the cube is built from the unit's greatest length, width and height, pallet and projections included.
  • Since 19 July 2025 the standard density scale has 13 steps: 8 up to 10 pounds per cubic foot is class 100, 35 up to 50 is class 55, and 50 or more is class 50.
  • Density sets the class only for items rated on that scale, so the NMFC item has to be confirmed in ClassIT+ before a calculated class is quoted to a carrier.

An LTL (less-than-truckload) carrier prices a shipment from a freight class, and for many commodities the class comes straight from one number: pounds per cubic foot. A pallet measured a few inches short can move the class by a full step, and the carrier's re-measure then turns the saving into a bill. Below: the density formula, the current 13-step scale, three worked examples that each end in a class, and how to measure a pallet so the number holds up.

What Freight Class Is

Freight class comes from the National Motor Freight Classification (NMFC), published by the National Motor Freight Traffic Association (NMFTA). The NMFC places each commodity in a class using four characteristics:

CharacteristicWhat it asks
DensityHow much the freight weighs for the space it occupies
StowabilityWhether it stacks, nests or leaves dead space in a trailer
HandlingHow much care or special equipment it needs on the dock and in the truck
LiabilityHow likely it is to be damaged, to damage other freight, or to be stolen

Classes run from 50 for the densest, easiest freight up to 500 for the lightest. A higher class means a higher charge for the same weight. The class is one input to the price: each carrier applies its own rates, discounts, minimum charges and accessorial fees, and those are outside this guide.

How Density Is Calculated

NMFTA's published method works on one handling unit, meaning a pallet, crate, carton or other unit as it is tendered to the carrier:

  1. Take the unit's greatest length, width and height in inches. Include the pallet and anything that sticks out.
  2. Multiply the three numbers, then divide by 1,728 (cubic inches in a cubic foot) to get cubic feet.
  3. Divide the unit's weight as tendered by the cubic feet. The answer is density in pounds per cubic foot (pcf).

NMFTA's own example is a pallet of boxes measuring 48" × 40" × 45" and weighing 450 lb. Volume is 86,400 cubic inches, which is 50 cubic feet, and 450 ÷ 50 gives 9.00 pcf.

Formula: density (pcf) = weight (lb) ÷ (length × width × height ÷ 1,728). Common mistakes: measuring the cargo and leaving out the pallet, using the pallet's nominal 48 × 40 when the load overhangs it, entering the weight of the cargo without the pallet, and mixing centimetres with inches.

The Standard Density Scale Since July 2025

NMFTA's Docket 2025-1 took effect on 19 July 2025. It expanded the standard density scale for freight with no handling, stowability or liability issues from 11 steps to 13 by adding class 55 and class 50 at the dense end. Which row applies to a given commodity depends on its NMFC item number, so look the item up in NMFTA's ClassIT+ rather than reading a class off this table.

Worked Example: Where the Measurement Changes the Class

A palletised load of packaged goods is stacked 52" long, 44" wide and 60" high including the pallet, and weighs 600 lb. Assume the commodity is rated on the standard density scale.

  1. Volume: 52 × 44 × 60 = 137,280 cubic inches, which is 79.44 cubic feet.
  2. Density: 600 ÷ 79.44 = 7.55 pcf.
  3. Scale row: 6 to less than 8 pcf, so class 125.

Now suppose the shipper measured only the cargo and left out a 6-inch pallet, entering 54" for height. Volume becomes 123,552 cubic inches, or 71.5 cubic feet, and density 600 ÷ 71.5 = 8.39 pcf. That is in the 8 to less than 10 row, class 100. The load looks one step cheaper than it is.

The effect on a bill, using invented rates for illustration only (a carrier's real rate comes from its tariff or contract): at $40 per hundredweight for class 100 and $50 for class 125, a 600 lb shipment rates 6 hundredweights, so $240 as quoted against $300 once the carrier measures the full 60" and reclassifies. The $60 difference is before any reclassification or inspection fee the carrier's terms add.

Multiple Handling Units

NMFC Item 110, Section 8(d) deals with shipments of several units. Density is calculated on each handling unit unless another rule provides otherwise. The exception: if the shipping papers show only one total weight for all the units, density may be calculated from the total weight and the total cube. NMFTA states that rule changes are outside the scope of Docket 2025-1, so the 2025 scale change did not alter this rule.

Take two pallets, both rated on the standard scale. Pallet A is 48" × 40" × 30" and weighs 400 lb: 33.33 cubic feet and 12.0 pcf, class 85. Pallet B is 48" × 40" × 60" and weighs 200 lb: 66.67 cubic feet and 3.0 pcf, class 250. If the bill of lading lists each pallet's weight, each is classed on its own density. If it lists only 600 lb for the shipment, the total cube is 100 cubic feet and the density is 6.0 pcf, class 125 for the whole shipment. Neither route is always cheaper, because the result depends on the carrier's rates. What matters is that the paperwork decides which calculation is allowed, so list each unit's weight and dimensions on the bill of lading.

Measuring So the Number Holds Up

SituationWhat to do
Standard box load on a palletMeasure the finished unit, including pallet and wrap, after it is built. Re-measure if the packaging changes.
Cartons overhang the palletUse the overhanging dimension, not the 48 × 40 of the pallet. The cube is what the carrier must reserve.
Irregular or tapered loadUse the greatest length, width and height, as if the unit sat in a box that just encloses it.
Anything sticking out (handles, fittings, banding)Include it. Projections count in the cube.
Measurement lands near a class boundaryNMFTA's own density calculator page notes a possible half-inch variation in dimensions, which can move a load across a boundary. Measure carefully and record how.
WeightWeigh the unit as tendered, pallet included, on a scale that can be shown to be accurate.

No source found for this guide says whether carriers round dimensions up, so ask the carrier how it rounds before comparing a calculated density against a boundary.

If a Carrier Reclassifies the Shipment

  1. Compare the carrier's measurements and weight with yours, unit by unit.
  2. Check the NMFC item number on the bill of lading against ClassIT+. A wrong item number is as likely a cause as a wrong measurement.
  3. Send the carrier your photographs, measurement record and bill of lading, since those are what a dispute turns on.
  4. For a disagreement about how an item is classified, NMFTA accepts requests for interpretation. It asks for photographs, dimensions, construction details and typical packaging.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does density set the class for every commodity?

No. It sets the class only for items rated on the standard density scale. Items with handling, stowability or liability issues have classes tied to those characteristics.

Where do I find my commodity's NMFC item number?

In ClassIT+, NMFTA's digital NMFC. NMFTA also offers a free lookup tool at getclassification.com for checking whether an item was affected by the 2025 changes.

Do classes 77.5, 110, 200 and 500 still exist?

Yes. They are not steps on the 13-step density scale, but they remain classes in the NMFC.

Does NMFTA's calculator give me the class?

No. It returns density. The class needs the NMFC item and its provisions.

A calculated density is 9.9 pcf. Is that class 100 or 92.5?

On the scale, 9.9 is below 10, so class 100. A load this close to 10 is one where measurement tolerance matters, so measure again and keep the record.
