Searches for "truck companies" and "freight carriers" are mostly people trying to find a carrier, and the hard part is not finding one but knowing whether it is legitimate. Federal rules make several things about a motor carrier a matter of public record or something it must be able to produce. This guide lists what to verify before you tender freight, what the rule says about each item, and where the rules themselves tell you to look. It does not list or rank companies. The same checks apply whether you are comparing large national trucking companies or small local carriers.

Safety Ratings, Explained

49 CFR 385.3 defines the rating terms. A satisfactory rating means the carrier has adequate safety management controls in place to meet the safety fitness standard, judged as appropriate to its size and type of operation. A conditional rating means it does not have adequate controls to ensure compliance with the standard, in a way that could result in the listed occurrences. An unsatisfactory rating means it lacks adequate controls and that has resulted in those occurrences. An unrated carrier is one to which FMCSA has not assigned a rating.

Two points are easy to misread. First, "unrated" does not mean "unsafe": the rule defines it only as a carrier that has not been rated. Second, the consequence of an unsatisfactory rating is spelled out in 385.13. A carrier rated unsatisfactory is generally prohibited from operating a commercial motor vehicle, with the prohibition starting on the 46th day after a notice of a proposed unsatisfactory rating for carriers hauling placarded hazardous materials or passengers, and on the 61st day for others, and FMCSA may allow up to 60 additional days where it finds a good-faith effort to improve. A Federal agency must not use an unsatisfactory-rated carrier.

Where to Look

The federal rule on unsatisfactory ratings itself tells readers where to find a carrier's current safety rating: the Safety and Fitness Electronic Records System website at safer.fmcsa.dot.gov, or by telephone at (800) 832-5660. To see whether operating authority has been granted, FMCSA's registration page points to its Licensing and Insurance website: enter the MC or USDOT number, open the carrier's record and look at its authority history. Confirm the lookup steps and what each tool shows on FMCSA's own site. For insurance, remember that 387.7(e) makes proof of financial responsibility public information that must be produced on reasonable request by a member of the public, so you can ask the carrier or its insurer for it directly.

Walking Through One Quote

Take a made-up quote. A shipper is quoted $1,850 to move a 12,000-pound load by "Northline Freight." The shipper wants to confirm who it is dealing with.

  1. Registration. The shipper looks up the company's USDOT number and finds it registered under the name "Northline Transport LLC", not "Northline Freight." The name on the rate confirmation must match the registered entity, and the insurance endorsement has to be in that exact name (387.15). The shipper asks which entity will actually haul the freight.
  2. Broker or carrier. The reply says the company does not own trucks and "arranges" the load. That is the broker definition. The shipper now needs the company's broker registration and $75,000 bond status, not just a carrier check, and should ask who the actual motor carrier will be.
  3. Insurance. The shipper requests proof of insurance for the actual carrier. The minimum for non-hazardous freight is $750,000; if the company cannot produce proof on reasonable request, that is a reason to stop.
  4. Safety rating. The actual carrier shows as "unrated". That is not a warning on its own, but it means no rating exists to rely on, so the shipper asks for other evidence of safety practices.
  5. Claims. The shipper asks for the claims filing period in writing and confirms it is at least nine months.

CSA crash and inspection data, fraud patterns such as double brokering and identity theft, and cargo insurance are separate topics, and no company is recommended or ranked here. The roles in a shipment are explained in Shipper, Carrier and Consignee: Who Does What in a Freight Shipment; insurance and claims are covered in Truck Insurance Requirements: The Federal Minimum Liability Levels and Freight Claims: Loss and Damage Deadlines and Carrier Duties.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I know a trucking company is legitimate?

Confirm it is registered and displays a USDOT number, that insurance is on file in its exact name at or above the federal minimum, and what its safety rating is. Each of these is something the federal rules make verifiable.

Where can I see a carrier's safety rating?

49 CFR 385.13 points to the FMCSA Safety and Fitness Electronic Records System at safer.fmcsa.dot.gov, or the telephone number (800) 832-5660.

Is an unrated carrier unsafe?

Not by definition. Under 385.3, unrated means FMCSA has not assigned a safety rating. It tells you only that no rating exists.

Can a carrier refuse to show proof of insurance?

The rule says proof of financial responsibility is public information and must be produced on reasonable request by a member of the public (387.7(e)).
