  • The customer that owes the invoice may keep paying the carrier until it receives an authenticated notice that the account was assigned and that payment goes to the factor, and after that notice only a payment to the factor discharges the debt (UCC 9-406(a)).
  • The cost of a deal comes from the contract and not the UCC, so compare offers on total dollars received per invoice; in the invented example below a 90 percent advance and a 3 percent fee turn a $2,000 invoice into $1,940.

A carrier delivers a load, invoices the broker or shipper, and waits. Factoring, also called load or trucker factoring, is one way to turn that unpaid invoice into cash sooner. Below: what factoring is in legal terms, what the notice of assignment does, what the customer can still argue after an assignment, and how the cost works. No companies are named, no financial advice is offered, and every fee in the examples is invented. The UCC text used here is the model version at Cornell LII; each State enacts its own, sometimes with changes, so check the version where you do business.

What Factoring Is, in Legal Terms

In factoring a business sells its unpaid invoices, which the UCC calls accounts, to a third party, the factor. Article 9 applies to a transaction that creates a security interest and also to a sale of accounts (UCC 9-109(a)(1), (3)). Section 9-318(a) adds that a debtor that has sold an account does not retain a legal or equitable interest in the collateral sold. After the sale the invoice belongs to the factor. The commercial terms, including the fee, the reserve and whether the carrier must make the factor whole if the customer never pays, come from the contract between carrier and factor.

The Notice of Assignment

The person who owes the invoice is the account debtor, here the broker or shipper. Section 9-406(a) says it may discharge its obligation by paying the assignor (the carrier) until, but not after, it receives a notification, authenticated by the assignor or the assignee, that the amount due has been assigned and that payment is to be made to the assignee. After receipt, it discharges the obligation by paying the assignee and not by paying the carrier. Four details follow from the text:

  • The notification is ineffective if it does not reasonably identify the rights assigned (9-406(b)(1)).
  • If the account debtor asks, the assignee must seasonably furnish reasonable proof that the assignment was made. Until it does, the account debtor may discharge its obligation by paying the carrier, even after the notice (9-406(c)).
  • The account debtor may, at its option, disregard a notice telling it to pay less than the full amount of a periodic payment, even if only part of the account was assigned (9-406(b)(3)).
  • A term in the contract between the customer and the carrier that bans or requires consent to assigning the account is generally ineffective to the extent it does so (9-406(d)), with exceptions found elsewhere in the UCC.

For a broker or shipper that receives such a notice, the practical step is to check that it identifies the specific invoices and to ask for proof of the assignment before redirecting payment.

What the Customer Can Still Argue

Under 9-404(a), unless the account debtor has made an enforceable agreement not to assert defenses or claims, the factor's rights are subject to all the terms of the agreement between the customer and the carrier and to any defense or claim in recoupment arising from the same transaction, and to any other defense or claim against the carrier that accrued before the customer received the notification. A claim can reduce what the customer owes the factor, but under 9-404(b) it is asserted only to reduce the amount owed. In a freight setting that means a cargo damage claim from the same shipment can reduce the invoice the factor collects, which is one reason contracts spell out who bears that loss.

What a $2,000 Invoice Nets (Invented)

A carrier invoices a broker $2,000. The factoring contract provides an advance of 90 percent and a fee of 3 percent of the invoice, with the rest held as a reserve.

  • Advance paid to the carrier: 0.90 × $2,000 = $1,800.
  • Fee: 0.03 × $2,000 = $60.
  • Reserve released after the broker pays the factor: $2,000 − $1,800 − $60 = $140.
  • Total received: $1,800 + $140 = $1,940, which is $60 less than the invoice.
  • Fee as a share of the cash received up front: $60 ÷ $1,800 = 3.3 percent. If the broker would have paid 30 days later, the fee is $2 for each day the cash arrives early.

Formula: advance = invoice × advance rate; fee = invoice × fee rate; reserve released = invoice − advance − fee; total received = advance + reserve. Common mistakes: comparing headline percentages instead of dollars, forgetting fees for payment methods or late customer payment, and assuming the carrier is free of the risk of non-payment without reading the contract.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does the broker have to pay the factor instead of me?

After it receives an authenticated notice that the account was assigned and that payment goes to the factor, it discharges the debt only by paying the factor (9-406(a)). It may ask for reasonable proof of the assignment first (9-406(c)).

Is factoring a loan?

The UCC puts a sale of accounts within Article 9 and says the seller keeps no interest in what was sold (9-109(a)(3), 9-318(a)). Whether a particular arrangement is a sale or a loan can depend on its terms, which is a question for a lawyer reading the contract.

Is quick pay the same as factoring?

No. Quick pay is an accelerated payment term from the party that owes the invoice; factoring sells the invoice to a third party. "Quick pay" is not a legal term, so get the terms and any fee in writing.
