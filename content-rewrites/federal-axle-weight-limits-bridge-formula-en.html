• Interstate weight is checked in layers: 20,000 pounds on a single axle, 34,000 on a tandem and 80,000 gross, then the Bridge Formula caps every group of two or more consecutive axles on top of those.
  • On a five-axle tractor-trailer FHWA treats axle groups 1 to 3, 1 to 5 and 2 to 5 as the critical checks, and in the worked example below it is group 2 to 5 that fails, by 2,000 pounds.
  • A load can pass every axle weight and still be illegal, because spacing sets the limit as much as weight does; sliding the trailer tandem back or removing weight are the two ways to fix a failing group.

The 80,000-pound gross limit is the number everyone remembers, but a truck under 80,000 pounds can still be overweight if too much of that load sits on a short run of axles. The federal rule for the Interstate System has four parts that all have to pass: single axle, tandem, gross, and the Bridge Formula applied to every group of axles. Below are the limits, the formula, a five-axle truck checked group by group, and what to change when one group fails.

The Limits at a Glance

23 CFR 658.17 applies to the Interstate System and reasonable access to it. Off the Interstate, weight limits are set by each State.

Two definitions decide which limit applies to a pair of axles. A single axle is the weight carried by wheels whose centers fall within 40 inches of each other. A tandem is two or more consecutive axles whose centers are more than 40 and no more than 96 inches apart (23 CFR 658.5). Axles further apart than 96 inches are governed by the Bridge Formula alone. FHWA's guidance gives an example: at 97 inches apart, two axles may carry 38,000 pounds, not 34,000.

The rule also tells States what they may not enforce on the Interstate. They may not set limits below 20,000 pounds on a single axle, 34,000 on a tandem or the Bridge Formula weight (up to 80,000 pounds), tolerances included. They may not limit tire load to less than 500 pounds per inch of tire or tread width, except on the steering axle, and may not limit a steering axle below 20,000 pounds or the manufacturer's axle rating, whichever is lower. The listed weights are inclusive of tolerances. The one allowance is for portable scales (wheel-load weighers), which may not exceed 5 percent. A fine schedule that charges nothing up to a set threshold, such as 1,000 pounds, is treated as a tolerance that 23 U.S.C. 127 does not authorize.

The Bridge Formula

The formula caps the weight on any group of two or more consecutive axles by how many axles there are and how far apart the outer two sit. Spreading weight over a longer distance stresses a bridge less, so a longer group may carry more. Congress enacted it in 1975 to limit the weight-to-length ratio of a vehicle crossing a bridge.

W = 500 × [ (L × N ÷ (N − 1)) + 12N + 36 ]

  • W is the most the group may weigh, to the nearest 500 pounds.
  • L is the distance in feet between the outer axles of the group.
  • N is the number of axles in the group.

Rounding has one detail that matters at the edges. FHWA's web page says only "to the nearest 500 pounds". The pamphlet's Bridge Table adds a footnote: its values reflect FHWA's policy of rounding down when calculated weights fall exactly halfway between 500-pound increments (FHWA-HOP-19-028, footnote 1 to the table, printed pages 5 and 6, which is page 4 of the 6-page PDF). For three axles spaced 9 feet, the formula gives 42,750 and the table shows 42,500. The regulation itself does not state a rounding rule for exact halves, so a result of this kind is worth confirming with the enforcing agency. The calculator below shows both numbers when it hits a half.

The rule carves out one exception. Two consecutive sets of tandem axles may each carry 34,000 pounds if the overall distance from the first axle to the last axle of those sets is 36 feet or more. The total may never exceed 80,000 pounds.

Check the Formula With the Tandem Limit

Two axles (N = 2) spaced 4 feet apart give W = 500 × [ (4 × 2 ÷ 1) + 24 + 36 ] = 34,000, which is the tandem limit in 658.17(d). The formula and the tandem limit agree where they meet.

Common mistakes: measuring L to the wrong axle, using the distance between the king pin and the rear axle instead of axle to axle, counting only the whole truck and skipping the inner groups, and forgetting that the single and tandem limits still apply after the formula passes.

Worked Example: A Five-Axle Truck, Every Group

A five-axle tractor-semitrailer has the steer axle (axle 1), a drive tandem (axles 2 and 3) and a trailer tandem (axles 4 and 5). Measured from axle 1, the axles sit at 0, 15, 19, 44 and 48 feet. The steer axle carries 12,000 pounds and each of the other four carries 16,500, for a gross of 78,000 pounds.

First the single, tandem and gross limits. The steer axle at 12,000 pounds is under 20,000. Each tandem is 4 feet (48 inches) apart, inside the 40-to-96-inch range, and carries 33,000, under 34,000. The gross of 78,000 is under 80,000. Those pass. Now the Bridge Formula for every group of consecutive axles:

Three of the formula maximums (groups 1 to 3, 2 to 4 and 3 to 5) fall exactly halfway between 500-pound steps and are rounded down as in FHWA's table. Rounding them up would add 500 pounds to each limit and change no result.

Group 2 to 5 is the limit. The two tandems are 33 feet apart, short of the 36 feet the tandem exception needs, so the formula applies and allows 64,000 pounds against 66,000 actual. The truck is under 80,000 gross and under every axle limit, and it is still overweight.

FHWA's guidance names axle combinations 1 to 3, 1 to 5 and 2 to 5 as the critical ones for this vehicle type, and says that if those are satisfactory the others normally will be. The table above checks all ten groups anyway, because that is what the rule requires.

Fixing a Failing Group

FixEffect in the example
Slide the trailer tandem back 3 ft, if the trailer's slide rails allow itL for axles 2 to 5 becomes 36 ft. The two-tandem exception now applies (each tandem may carry 34,000), and the formula itself gives 66,000 at 36 ft, which equals the actual weight. Axles 1 to 5 then span 51 ft and may carry 80,000, still above the 78,000 actual.
Remove at least 2,000 lb from the tandemsThe 2 to 5 group drops to 64,000 and passes, as long as the other groups still pass.
Shift cargo toward the steer axleLowers the tandem weights. The steer axle has room up to 20,000 lb, but check that the drive tandem still carries enough for traction and that the front axle does not exceed its rated capacity.
Add an axleMore axles raise the formula weight, but the axle group must still pass every check.

Re-weigh after any change. Moving a trailer tandem or cargo shifts weight between axles, so a fix for one group can break another.

Exceptions and Special Cases in the Rule

  • Special permits. States may issue permits without regard to the axle, gross or Bridge Formula limits for nondivisible vehicles or loads (658.17(h)).
  • Grandfathered weights. The single, tandem and gross limits do not apply to weights legally authorized under State law on 1 July 1956. The group-of-axles requirement does not apply to vehicles grandfathered under State tables or formulas on 4 January 1975. Because of this, some States allow more than the federal figures on their Interstate highways (658.17(i); FHWA guidance).
  • Idle-reduction equipment. A vehicle with an auxiliary power or idle-reduction unit may carry up to an additional 400 pounds in the gross, axle, tandem or bridge-formula limits, or the certified weight of the unit if less. The operator must show the unit's weight by written certification and that the technology is fully functional at all times (658.17(n)).
  • Named locations. The rule exempts certain specialised coal, log and pulpwood vehicles on Interstate 68 in Allegany and Garrett Counties, Maryland, and vehicles that could legally operate on a section of Interstate 99 in Pennsylvania before 29 December 1995 (658.17(j) and (m)).

Scope: these are federal limits for the Interstate System. Permits, routing, escorts, fine amounts and State limits on other roads vary by State, and this guide does not list them. Weight is one of several things an inspector can check; Commercial Vehicle Roadside Inspections: What Each Level Covers covers the rest, and Cargo Securement covers how the load is held.

Frequently Asked Questions

Where do I measure L?

From the center of the first axle of the group to the center of the last axle. For the whole five-axle truck that is the steer axle to the rear trailer axle, not the king pin to the rear axle.

Why does a short truck with many axles still fail?

Because the formula weight rises with spacing as well as axle count. Several closely spaced axles are the usual trouble: in FHWA's single-unit truck example, three axles at 15,000 pounds each over 9 feet carry 45,000 against 42,500 allowed.

Does the 80,000-pound limit apply on every road?

No. This rule governs the Interstate System and reasonable access to it. Other roads follow State limits.

Is there a scale tolerance?

The rule allows none except for portable scales, where an allowance may not exceed 5 percent. A fine schedule that charges nothing below a threshold counts as an unauthorized tolerance.
