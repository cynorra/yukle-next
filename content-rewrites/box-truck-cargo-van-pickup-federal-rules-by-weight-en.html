Box trucking, cargo van hauling and pickup freight look similar from the driver's seat, but the federal rules that apply change at specific weight lines, and some of the lines are easy to miss. The most common surprise is a pickup that weighs less than 10,001 pounds but becomes a regulated commercial vehicle the moment it pulls a trailer. This guide sets out the thresholds as they appear in the regulations, works through four example vehicles, and flags the one question the regulations alone do not answer. Whether you are starting a box trucking business or hauling with a Sprinter-type cargo van, the same weight lines decide which rules apply.

How the 10,001-Pound Line Works

The definition of a commercial motor vehicle in 390.5 uses the greater of the rating and the actual weight. The rule says the vehicle must have a gross vehicle weight rating (GVWR) or gross combination weight rating (GCWR), or an actual gross vehicle weight or gross combination weight, of 10,001 pounds or more, whichever is greater. The GCWR is the greater of the manufacturer's value shown on the certification label or the sum of the GVWRs (or actual weights) of the power unit and the towed unit, using whichever combination produces the highest value. There is one exception: the power unit's own GCWR is not used to define a commercial motor vehicle when it is not towing another vehicle.

That last sentence is why a heavy-duty pickup with a high GCWR on its label is not automatically regulated when it is driven without a trailer, but the same pickup towing a trailer is judged as a combination.

Four Vehicles, Four Answers

These are made-up vehicles, not specific models. Each example assumes the vehicle is used in interstate commerce to transport property and is not carrying placarded hazardous materials.

  1. A cargo van rated at 9,500 lb GVWR, no trailer. It is under 10,001 pounds, so it is not a commercial motor vehicle under 390.5 on weight alone. Subchapter B applies to commercial motor vehicles (390.3(a)), so the safety rules and the USDOT registration tied to them do not apply on that basis. No CDL is needed, since it is far below 26,001 pounds.
  2. A pickup rated at 9,900 lb GVWR pulling a trailer rated at 7,000 lb GVWR. Each unit is under 10,001 pounds, but the combination is not measured unit by unit. The sum of the ratings is 9,900 + 7,000 = 16,900 lb, which is above 10,001, so the combination is a commercial motor vehicle when used to transport property in interstate commerce. A CDL is still not needed, because the combination is under 26,001 pounds.
  3. A box truck rated at 16,000 lb GVWR. It is above 10,001 pounds, so it is a commercial motor vehicle. A for-hire carrier moving non-hazardous property in it falls in the $750,000 row of 387.9. It does not need a CDL for weight alone because 16,000 is below 26,001.
  4. A box truck rated at 26,001 lb GVWR. It reaches the heavy straight vehicle line in 383.5, so a Group B CDL is required, in interstate and intrastate commerce.

Two further triggers can override the weight analysis. A vehicle used to transport hazardous materials in a quantity requiring placards is a commercial motor vehicle regardless of its weight (390.5), and a vehicle of any size used to transport hazardous materials, as 383.5 defines them, falls into the CDL Group C category. See Hazmat Placarding: Table 1, Table 2 and the 1,001-Pound Rule for when placards are required, and CDL Classes and Endorsements: What Each One Covers for the groups.

Operating Authority Is a Separate Question

The 10,001-pound line decides whether the federal safety rules and the USDOT number apply. Operating authority, the registration FMCSA issues to for-hire carriers, has its own test. FMCSA's registration page says that, in general, companies that transport federally regulated commodities owned by others, or arrange their transport, for a fee in interstate commerce need interstate operating authority (a docket number, often called an MC number) in addition to a USDOT number. It lists three groups that do not need authority: private carriers that haul their own cargo, for-hire carriers that haul only exempt commodities, and carriers that operate entirely within a federally designated commercial zone.

The statute behind this, 49 U.S.C. 13901, says a person may provide transportation as a motor carrier subject to the Act only if registered, and defines a motor carrier as a person providing motor vehicle transportation for compensation, with no weight stated. The exemptions in 49 U.S.C. 13506 include a farmer hauling the farmer's own agricultural products and the transportation of ordinary livestock and agricultural commodities. FMCSA's page does not single out vehicles under 10,001 pounds, so a for-hire van or pickup operator should ask FMCSA how the authority requirement applies to them before taking freight for pay.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does a cargo van under 10,001 pounds need a USDOT number?

The federal safety rules and the USDOT registration in 390.201 apply to motor carriers subject to the safety rules, which cover commercial motor vehicles. A van under 10,001 pounds is not one on weight alone, but a van that carries placarded hazardous materials, or that pulls a trailer so the combined rating reaches 10,001 pounds, can be. Check your own configuration against 390.5.

Can a pickup become a commercial motor vehicle when towing?

Yes. The combination is measured by the greater of the manufacturer's GCWR or the sum of the unit ratings or actual weights. A pickup rated at 9,900 pounds pulling a trailer rated at 7,000 pounds totals 16,900 pounds.

Do I need a CDL for a 16,000-pound box truck?

Not for weight alone. The CDL lines in 383.5 start at 26,001 pounds for a single vehicle, unless the vehicle carries 16 or more passengers or hazardous materials.

How much liability insurance does a for-hire box truck need?

For non-hazardous property in a vehicle rated 10,001 pounds or more, the federal minimum in 387.9 is $750,000. Shippers and brokers can require more by contract.

Do I need a USDOT number to start a box trucking business?

It depends on the vehicle. Under 49 CFR 390.201, motor carriers subject to the federal safety rules must register with FMCSA, and those rules apply to commercial motor vehicles, which includes a box truck rated 10,001 pounds or more that is used in interstate commerce. Whether a for-hire operation also needs operating authority is a separate question, covered above.
