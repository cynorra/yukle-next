• A vehicle is a federal commercial motor vehicle only when it is used in interstate commerce and the greater of its rating or actual weight is 10,001 lb or more, or it carries placarded hazardous materials, and a combination is measured by its combined rating, so a 9,900 lb pickup towing a 7,000 lb trailer counts as 16,900 lb.
  • CDL thresholds are separate: 26,001 lb for a single vehicle (Group B) or for a combination whose towed unit is over 10,000 lb (Group A), and the CDL rules apply to intrastate driving as well.
  • Hazardous materials change the answer at any size, because a placarded load makes a vehicle a commercial motor vehicle and puts it in CDL Group C.

Box trucking, cargo van hauling and pickup freight look alike from the driver's seat, but the federal rules that apply change at specific weight lines and with the kind of trip. The most common surprise is a pickup under 10,001 lb that becomes a regulated vehicle the moment it pulls a trailer. Below are the tests as the regulations state them, a selector to run your own vehicle, and eight worked cases.

The tests overlap but do not match. A 16,000 lb box truck is a commercial motor vehicle (so the safety rules and the insurance floor apply) but needs no CDL. The same truck driven only within one State is not a federal commercial motor vehicle under 390.5, though State rules may apply, and the 26,001 lb CDL line still applies to it wherever it drives.

Run Your Own Vehicle

Rules used: weight = the greater of the rating and the actual weight, with a combination measured by the sum of the unit ratings; CMV needs interstate commerce; CDL groups per 383.5. Common mistakes: testing a pickup and its trailer separately, reading the weight from the registration instead of the certification label, and assuming the CDL line and the 10,001 lb line are the same.

How the Weight Line Works

GVWR is the value the manufacturer specifies as the maximum loaded weight of a single vehicle, and it is printed on the vehicle's certification label. Under 49 CFR 567.4 that label is on the hinge pillar, door-latch post or door edge next to the driver's seat (or, if those are impractical, other listed spots near the driver). The 390.5 test uses the greater of the rating and the vehicle's actual weight.

For a combination, 390.5 defines GCWR as the greater of two values: the figure the power-unit manufacturer displays on its certification label, or the sum of the GVWRs or actual weights of the power unit and the towed unit, whichever combination is highest. There is one exception: the power unit's own GCWR is not used to define a commercial motor vehicle when it is not towing. That is why a heavy-duty pickup with a high GCWR on its label is not regulated on that figure alone while it runs without a trailer, and is judged as a combination once it tows.

Hazardous Materials Override the Weight

390.5 makes a vehicle a commercial motor vehicle when it transports hazardous material in a quantity requiring placards, whatever its weight. 383.5 puts a vehicle of any size used for that purpose in CDL Group C if it does not already fall in Group A or B. The 383.5 definition of hazardous materials is narrower than "anything labelled hazardous": it means material required to be placarded under subpart F of part 172, or any quantity of a listed select agent or toxin. For when placards are required, see Hazmat Placarding; for the CDL groups and endorsements, CDL Classes and Endorsements.

Operating Authority Is a Separate Question

The 10,001 lb line decides whether the safety rules and the USDOT number apply. Operating authority is a different registration. 49 U.S.C. 13901 says a person may provide transportation as a motor carrier, freight forwarder or broker, within the Act's jurisdiction, only if registered. The statute defines a motor carrier as a person providing motor vehicle transportation for compensation (13102), with no weight line, and its exemptions in 13506(a) are by operation, for example a farmer's own agricultural commodities and supplies, and the transportation of ordinary livestock or of agricultural commodities other than manufactured products. FMCSA's registration pages explain who needs a docket number in practice. They could not be re-read for this guide, so a for-hire van or pickup operator should confirm with FMCSA before taking freight for pay.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does a cargo van under 10,001 lb need a USDOT number?

Not on weight alone under the federal rules, because the safety rules cover commercial motor vehicles. A van that carries placarded hazardous materials, or tows so the combined weight reaches 10,001 lb, can be one. A State may have its own number or registration, which this guide did not check.

Where do I find the GVWR?

On the certification label by the driver's seat, per 567.4. Registration papers and sales listings may show other figures.

Does a CDL apply to a 26,000 lb truck?

Not by weight: Group B starts at 26,001 lb. Towing a unit over 10,000 lb that takes the combination to 26,001 lb or more makes it Group A.

What changes if I only drive inside my State?

The federal commercial motor vehicle definition requires interstate commerce, so the federal safety rules do not apply on weight alone. The CDL rules in part 383 still apply intrastate. State rules differ and were not checked.

Scope: this is a reading of the federal definitions, not legal advice, and State rules, exemptions and short-haul provisions are outside it. Hours of service for drivers of these vehicles are in Hours of Service Limits.
