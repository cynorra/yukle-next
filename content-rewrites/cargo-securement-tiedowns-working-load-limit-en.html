• Every tied-down load faces two tests: a minimum number of tiedowns set by the article's length (49 CFR 393.110) and an aggregate working load limit of at least half the article's weight (393.106(d)), and blocking the load lowers only the first.
  • A tiedown counts for its full working load limit only when it crosses the article to an anchor on the other side of the vehicle; a tiedown from a vehicle anchor to an article anchor, or looped back to the same side, counts for half.
  • The driver must check the load within the first 50 miles and again at each change of duty status, after 3 hours or after 150 miles of driving, whichever comes first (392.9).

A load can have enough tiedowns and still be under-secured, because the federal rules ask how many tiedowns and how strong they are in total, and a load must pass both. The rules below follow the order a loader would apply them.

What the Rule Requires

Section 393.100 applies to trucks, truck tractors, semitrailers, full trailers and pole trailers. Cargo must be secured so it cannot leak, spill, blow or fall off, and cannot shift enough to affect the vehicle's stability or maneuverability. Under 393.106(b) it must be firmly immobilized or secured by structures of adequate strength, dunnage, shoring bars, tiedowns or a combination. The section covers all articles of cargo except bulk commodities without structure or fixed shape, such as liquids, grain or gravel, carried in a tank, hopper or box that is part of the vehicle. Where 393.116 to 393.136 add requirements for a specific commodity, those take precedence.

Test 1: How Many Tiedowns?

Section 393.110 sets a minimum count from the article's length. If the article is not blocked against forward movement by a headerboard, bulkhead, other positioned cargo or another blocking device:

ArticleMinimum tiedowns
5 ft or less, 1,100 lb or less1
5 ft or less, more than 1,100 lb2
Over 5 ft up to 10 ft, any weight2
Over 10 ft2, plus 1 for every 10 ft or fraction beyond the first 10 ft (so 3 up to 20 ft, 4 up to 30 ft)

If the article is blocked, braced or immobilized against forward movement, it needs one tiedown for every 10 ft of length or fraction (393.110(c)). The count rules do not apply to a vehicle carrying machinery or fabricated structural items such as beams, girders or crane booms that must be fastened by special methods, but the cargo must still be securely and adequately fastened (393.110(d)).

Test 2: How Strong in Total?

Under 393.106(d), the aggregate working load limit (WLL) of the tiedowns securing an article must be at least one-half of the article's weight. Each tiedown contributes according to how it is run:

The WLL of a tiedown is the lowest WLL of any of its components, including the tensioner, or of the anchor points it attaches to (393.108(a)). Use the manufacturer's marking when there is one. The rule's defaults apply only to unmarked material: unmarked welded steel chain is treated as grade 30 proof coil chain, unmarked synthetic cordage as polypropylene fiber rope, and unmarked synthetic webbing takes its value from the width table. Calculator

Formula: required aggregate WLL = weight ÷ 2; each tiedown counts for its WLL (opposite side) or half of it (anchor-to-article or same-side loop); tiedowns needed = the larger of the length minimum and required aggregate WLL ÷ counted WLL, rounded up. Common mistakes: using the strap's breaking strength instead of its WLL, using the strap's rating when the anchor point is weaker, and counting a same-side loop at full value.

Condition of the Equipment

Section 393.104 adds rules that no count or rating can satisfy. Tiedowns, anchor points, headerboards and other securement parts must be in proper working order, with no cracks, cuts or other damage that reduces their working load limit. Tiedowns must not contain knots, must be attached so they cannot loosen or release in transit, and must have edge protection wherever they could be abraded or cut by the cargo. A repaired tiedown must be repaired to the applicable standard or the manufacturer's instructions. Articles likely to roll must be restrained by chocks, wedges or a cradle that cannot come loose (393.106(c)).

The performance standard behind the numbers is in 393.102. Tiedowns must withstand 0.8 g forward, 0.5 g rearward and 0.5 g sideways without exceeding their breaking strength, and the forces must stay within the working load limit at 0.435 g forward, 0.5 g rearward and 0.25 g sideways, each applied separately. An article not fully contained within the vehicle's structure needs securement that provides a downward force of at least 20 percent of its weight.

The Driver's Checks Under 392.9

Before driving, the driver of a truck or tractor must confirm the cargo is properly distributed and secured. The driver must then inspect the cargo and securement within the first 50 miles of the trip and adjust as needed, including adding tiedowns. After that, the driver must re-examine and adjust at a change of duty status, after 3 hours of driving, or after 150 miles, whichever comes first. The re-examination rules do not apply to the driver of a sealed vehicle who has been ordered not to open it or whose load makes inspection impracticable. A hypothetical case: a driver who leaves at 06:00 and stops for a 30-minute break at 09:00 has a duty-status change that triggers a check, so the 3-hour mark and the break fall together. For how securement fits into an inspection, see Commercial Vehicle Roadside Inspections, and for weight, Federal Truck Weight Limits.

Frequently Asked Questions

If I meet the minimum number of tiedowns, am I compliant?

Not automatically. The count in 393.110 is in addition to the aggregate WLL rule in 393.106(d), and both must be met.

What if my strap has no WLL marking?

Webbing then takes the table value for its width. Chain, cordage, wire rope and steel strapping have their own default rules in 393.108 and 393.104.

Does a headerboard reduce the strength I need?

The rule text lowers only the count. The aggregate WLL requirement in 393.106(d) is stated for tiedowns used to secure the article and does not change with blocking.

Can a knotted strap be used if it is tight?

No. 393.104(f)(1) says tiedowns and securing devices must not contain knots.

Scope: these are the general rules. Commodity-specific rules in 393.116 to 393.136 (logs, metal coils, concrete pipe, heavy vehicles and others) add requirements, and none of this is legal advice.
