Freight is full of terms that people use loosely: carrier, shipper, broker, bill of lading, LTL, accessorial. Some are defined in federal law, some are defined by NMFTA's classification system, and some, like detention and deadhead, are not defined by either and mean whatever a contract says. This freight terms glossary separates the three. Each entry gives the meaning as the source states it, in plain wording, with the source named, so you can tell a legal definition from industry usage.

Terms Defined in Federal Law or Regulation

TermMeaningSource
Bill of ladingA receipt (or bill of lading) a for-hire motor carrier issues for property tendered for transportation. It must show consignor and consignee names, origin and destination, number of packages, description of the freight, and weight, volume or measurement where applicable to rating.49 CFR 373.101
BrokerA person, other than a motor carrier or its employee or agent, that sells, negotiates for, or arranges transportation by motor carrier for compensation.49 U.S.C. 13102(2); 49 CFR 371.2
CarrierA motor carrier, a water carrier, or a freight forwarder.49 U.S.C. 13102(3)
Commercial motor vehicle (CMV)A vehicle used on a highway in interstate commerce to transport passengers or property that meets one of the weight, passenger or hazardous materials tests, including a rating or actual weight of 10,001 pounds or more.49 CFR 390.5
Consignor, consigneeThe sender and receiver named on the bill of lading and freight bill. The regulations use these terms; "shipper" is not defined in 13102.49 CFR 373.101, 373.103
Driver vehicle inspection report (DVIR)A written report at the end of each day's work identifying the vehicle and any defect affecting safe operation, signed by the driver.49 CFR 396.11
For-hire motor carrierA person engaged in the transportation of goods or passengers for compensation.49 CFR 390.5
Freight bill (expense bill)The bill a for-hire carrier issues for each shipment, showing the rates assessed and total charges due. It goes to the shipper or receiver owing the charges.49 CFR 373.103
Freight forwarderA person holding itself out to the public to provide transportation of property for compensation that assembles and consolidates shipments, assumes responsibility for the transportation from receipt to destination, and uses a carrier for part of it.49 U.S.C. 13102(8)
Gross vehicle weight rating (GVWR)The manufacturer's maximum loaded weight of a single motor vehicle.49 CFR 390.5
Gross combination weight rating (GCWR)The greater of the manufacturer's value on the certification label or the sum of the GVWRs (or actual weights) of the power unit and towed units.49 CFR 390.5
In bulkTransporting property as cargo in containment systems with capacities above 3,500 water gallons (with separate definitions for some explosives and gases).49 CFR 387.5
Interstate commerceTrade or transportation between a place in a State and a place outside it, between two places in a State through another State or a place outside the United States, or between two places in a State as part of transportation that originates or terminates outside the State or the United States.49 CFR 390.5
Motor carrierA person providing motor vehicle transportation for compensation.49 U.S.C. 13102(14)
Out of serviceThe status of a vehicle declared and marked by an inspector when its condition or loading would likely cause an accident or breakdown. It may not be operated until listed repairs are completed.49 CFR 396.9
Private motor carrierA person who transports property or passengers by commercial motor vehicle and is not a for-hire motor carrier.49 CFR 390.5
Public liabilityLiability for bodily injury or property damage, including liability for environmental restoration.49 CFR 387.5
Record of duty status (RODS)The driver's record of duty status for each 24-hour period; the driver must keep a copy for the previous 7 consecutive days.49 CFR 395.8
Working load limit (WLL)The rating of a tiedown, which is the lowest rating of any of its components or of the anchor points it is attached to.49 CFR 393.108

Terms From the Freight Classification System

These come from the National Motor Freight Traffic Association (NMFTA), which publishes the classification system LTL pricing uses. They are industry standards, not federal law.

TermMeaning (NMFTA)
Freight classA class from 50 to 500 assigned to a commodity based on four transportation characteristics: density, handling, stowability and liability.
DensityHow heavy the freight is relative to the space it takes up, measured in pounds per cubic foot.
LTL (less-than-truckload)Freight moved with other shippers' freight on a shared trailer; generally one to six pallets.
FTL (full truckload)One trailer contracted to a single shipper or consignee; typically a load that fills a 53-foot dry van.
AccessorialAn added service on top of the base charge. NMFTA gives liftgate service and inside delivery as examples.

For the calculations behind these, see LTL Shipping: How Freight Class and Density Are Calculated and LTL Shipping Explained: How It Differs From Parcel and Full Truckload.

Terms With No Federal Definition

These are common in the industry but are not defined in the federal sources used for this guide, so their meaning depends on the contract or the company using them:

  • Detention, a charge for waiting time at a pickup or delivery.
  • Deadhead, driving without a load, usually to reach the next pickup.
  • Dispatcher. Not defined in 49 U.S.C. 13102. See What Is a Freight Broker? The Legal Definition and Requirements.
  • Lumper, a worker hired to unload, and the fee for that service.
  • Quick pay, an accelerated payment option. For how it differs from factoring, see Freight Factoring Explained: How Selling Invoices Works.

When a contract uses one of these words, ask for the definition in writing: the hour at which detention starts, who pays a lumper, what counts as an empty mile.
