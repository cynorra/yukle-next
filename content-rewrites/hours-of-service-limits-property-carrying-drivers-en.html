• The 14-hour period starts when a driver comes on duty after 10 consecutive hours off and does not pause for breaks, so a long wait at a dock can end the driving day before the 11 driving hours are used.
  • A 30-minute interruption is required only once more than 8 hours of driving have passed without one, and on-duty-not-driving time counts, so fueling and inspecting the truck can satisfy it.
  • The two short-haul exceptions both use a 150 air-mile radius but waive different things: the CDL version waives the logbook if the driver is released within 14 hours, and the non-CDL version waives the 14-hour limit but caps driving at the 14th hour on five days and the 16th hour on two days of any seven.

The federal hours-of-service rules for drivers of property-carrying commercial motor vehicles come down to a handful of numbers and a handful of exceptions. The numbers run on different clocks: the 11-hour driving limit counts only time at the controls, while the 14-hour period counts every hour from coming on duty. The sections below give each limit with its rule, two hour-by-hour examples, the weekly limit, the sleeper-berth split, and the exceptions that change the answer.

Two points cause most mistakes. The 14-hour period is a wall-clock limit: waiting, loading and traffic all use it up. And the 30-minute interruption does not have to be off duty. The rule lets it be off-duty time, sleeper-berth time, on-duty-not-driving time, or a combination.

What counts as on duty is broad. Under 49 CFR 395.2 it includes time waiting at a shipper's or carrier's facility to be dispatched unless the driver has been relieved from duty, time inspecting or servicing the vehicle, loading and unloading (including supervising or waiting during it), and time spent on a disabled vehicle. All of that counts toward the 14-hour period and the weekly limit, even though none of it is driving.

Formula: the 14-hour period ends at start time + 14 hours; driving left = 11 − hours driven; hours until a break = 8 − hours driven since the last 30-minute interruption; weekly hours left = 60 or 70 − hours on duty in the period. Common mistakes: counting the 14 hours from the first drive instead of from coming on duty, forgetting that a shipper wait is on-duty time, and assuming a break pauses the 14-hour period.

Example 1: A Clean Day

This is a hypothetical schedule, not a record of a real driver. A driver comes on duty at 06:00 after 10 consecutive hours off and drives without stopping.

  • 06:00 to 14:00: 8 hours of driving. Driving time has now passed 8 hours, so a 30-minute interruption is needed before more driving.
  • 14:00 to 14:30: 30-minute interruption, for example fueling and a vehicle inspection (on duty, not driving).
  • 14:30 to 17:30: 3 more hours of driving, making 11 for the day.

At 17:30 the driver has used the 11 hours and must stop driving, although the 14-hour period does not end until 20:00. The remaining two and a half hours can go to on-duty work that is not driving.

Example 2: A Day With a Dock Delay

Also hypothetical. A driver comes on duty at 05:00 and spends 3 hours at the shipper, loading and waiting. That is on-duty time, and the 14-hour period is already running, so it ends at 19:00. Driving starts at 08:00.

  • 08:00 to 16:00: 8 hours of driving.
  • 16:00 to 16:30: 30-minute interruption.
  • 16:30 to 19:00: the driver can drive until the 14-hour period ends, 2.5 hours.

Total driving is 10.5 hours. The driver stops half an hour short of the 11-hour limit because the 14-hour period closed first. The same schedule with no dock delay would have ended on driving time instead. This is why a late loading appointment costs driving hours even though nothing about the driving rule changed.

The Weekly Limit

The 60-hour or 70-hour limit counts on-duty time, not just driving. A carrier that operates every day of the week uses 70 hours in 8 consecutive days; a carrier that does not operate every day uses 60 hours in 7. The example below is hypothetical, with the 70-hour, 8-day rule:

After day 6 the driver has 65 hours and can work 5 more on day 7. At 70 the driver may not drive on day 8. On day 9 the window moves forward and day 1 drops out: days 2 to 8 hold 59 hours, so 11 hours become available again. A driver who does not want to wait for hours to roll off can restart the count by starting a period of 34 or more consecutive hours off duty, which ends the 7- or 8-day period under 395.3(c).

Splitting the Off-Duty Time in a Sleeper Berth

A driver using a sleeper berth may meet the 10-hour requirement in two periods rather than one. Under 395.1(g)(1)(ii) the two periods must meet all of these: neither is shorter than 2 consecutive hours, one is at least 7 consecutive hours in the sleeper berth, the two total at least 10 hours, and the driving before and after each period, added together, stays within the 11-hour and 14-hour limits. The rule also allows a combination of at least 7 consecutive hours in the sleeper berth and up to 3 hours riding in the passenger seat of the moving vehicle, immediately before or after, amounting to at least 10 consecutive hours.

Two things follow from a valid split. The 14-hour period does not include the qualifying rest periods, and the driving-time and 14-hour limits are recalculated from the end of the first of the two periods (395.1(g)(1)(iii)). The recalculation is where most errors happen, so a driver planning a split should have the ELD or a hand calculation confirm each limit rather than estimate.

Both short-haul exceptions also remove the 30-minute interruption requirement: 395.3(a)(3)(ii) does not apply to drivers who qualify for either. A driver using the non-CDL exception may not also use the sleeper-berth or 16-hour provisions. Other exceptions in 395.1, each with its own conditions, include:

  • Adverse driving conditions. Snow, ice, sleet, fog or other adverse weather, or unusual road or traffic conditions, that were not known and could not reasonably be known to the driver at the start of the duty day (or before driving after a qualifying rest) or to the carrier at dispatch. A driver who meets them and cannot safely finish the run may drive up to two additional hours to complete it or reach a place with safety for the occupants and security for the vehicle and cargo (395.1(b)(1)). A condition the driver could have anticipated does not qualify.
  • Emergency conditions. A driver may complete a run if it reasonably could have been completed absent the emergency (395.1(b)(2)).
  • Oilfield operations. For vehicles used exclusively in oilfield service, an 8-day period may end with an off-duty period of 24 or more successive hours (395.1(d)(1)).
  • Construction materials and ground-water drilling. A 7- or 8-day period may end with 24 or more successive hours off duty (395.1(l), (m)). "Construction materials" has a defined meaning in 395.2, including a 75 air-mile radius.
  • Retail store deliveries. From 10 to 25 December, drivers making local deliveries from retail stores to consumers within 100 air miles are exempt from 395.3(a) and (b) (395.1(f)).
  • Break-only exceptions. The 30-minute interruption does not apply to the interstate transport of livestock while on the vehicle or of commercial bees while on the vehicle (395.1(u), (v)).
  • Travel time. Time traveling at the carrier's direction, not driving, counts as on duty unless the driver gets at least 10 consecutive hours off duty on arrival (395.1(j)).

Alaska and Hawaii have their own provisions (395.1(h), (i)). Whether an exception applies depends on the details of the operation, so check the full text of 395.1 before relying on one.

Records: ELDs, Paper and Roadside

Under 49 CFR 395.8, a motor carrier must require each driver to record duty status for each 24-hour period, and since 18 December 2017 that means an ELD. A carrier may instead use paper logs for a driver who needs a record of duty status on no more than 8 days in any 30-day period, in certain driveaway-towaway operations, or in a vehicle manufactured before model year 2000 as shown by the VIN on the registration. The driver must submit each record to the carrier within 13 days, the carrier keeps records and supporting documents for 6 months, and the driver must have a copy of the previous 7 consecutive days available for inspection while on duty. A false entry or tampering with an ELD is prohibited. For malfunctions, exemptions and what to do during an inspection, see the ELD guide and Commercial Vehicle Roadside Inspections.

Drivers of passenger-carrying vehicles follow different limits in 49 CFR 395.5: 10 hours of driving and a 15-hour on-duty limit after 8 consecutive hours off duty.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does the 14-hour period stop when I take a break?

No. Under 395.3(a)(2) it runs for 14 consecutive hours after coming on duty. The one exception is a qualifying sleeper-berth split, where 395.1(g)(1)(iii)(B) excludes the qualifying rest periods from the 14-hour period.

Does waiting at a shipper count as on duty?

Yes, unless the carrier has relieved the driver from duty. It counts toward the 14-hour period and the weekly limit but not toward the 11 hours of driving.

Can I drive two extra hours in bad weather?

Only for conditions that meet the definition in 395.2: not known and not reasonably knowable at the start of the duty day or at dispatch. The extra time is to finish the run or reach a safe place, not to extend a planned trip.

How do I reset the 60 or 70 hours?

Take an off-duty period of 34 or more consecutive hours. A 7- or 8-day period may end with the beginning of that period (395.3(c)).

Do I need a break at exactly 8 hours?

The limit is that driving is not permitted once more than 8 hours of driving time have passed without a 30-minute interruption. An earlier break resets that count, and so does any 30-minute stretch of off-duty, sleeper-berth or on-duty-not-driving time.
