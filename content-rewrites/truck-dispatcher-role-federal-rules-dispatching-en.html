• Federal law does not define a dispatcher, but 49 CFR 390.5 includes in "motor carrier" the employees responsible for assigning or dispatching drivers, so a carrier's duties under the safety rules reach the dispatcher's decisions.
  • A dispatch plan that needs more than 11 hours of driving in a duty period cannot be run as planned: at 55 mph, 640 miles is about 11.6 hours of driving before any loading, waiting or the 30-minute interruption.
  • Shippers, receivers and transportation intermediaries, as well as carriers, may not coerce a driver to violate the safety rules, and a driver has 90 days to file a written complaint with FMCSA (49 CFR 390.6, 386.12(c)).

Ask ten people what a truck dispatcher does and the answers differ: the person at a carrier who assigns loads to drivers, an outside service that finds freight for owner-operators, or the operations desk at a broker. Federal law does not define the word. What it defines is who answers for what a driver does behind the wheel, and a dispatcher's decisions sit inside that. Below are where the rules reach dispatching, a check for whether a day's loads can be run, and how a dispatch service differs from a broker.

A Job Title, Not a Legal Category

The statute that defines the parties to a shipment, 49 U.S.C. 13102, defines motor carriers, brokers and freight forwarders, and it has no entry for dispatcher. The safety regulations approach from the other side. In 49 CFR 390.5, "motor carrier" includes a motor carrier's agents, officers and representatives and employees responsible for hiring, supervising, training, assigning or dispatching drivers. A dispatcher who works for a carrier is therefore not a separate regulated party; the carrier's duties reach the dispatcher's decisions.

A driver who believes he or she was coerced may file a written complaint under 386.12(c). It must be filed with FMCSA no later than 90 days after the event, be signed, and contain the driver's contact details, the name and address of the person allegedly coercing, the regulations the driver says were violated, and a concise statement of the facts with the date of each alleged violation.

Can This Plan Be Run? A Check

Take an invented plan. A driver comes on duty at 06:00 after 10 consecutive hours off, and the dispatcher lines up loads totalling 640 driving miles. At an average of 55 mph that is 640 ÷ 55 = 11.6 hours of driving. The limit is 11 hours of driving within the 14-hour period (395.3(a)), so the plan cannot be finished in one duty period, before a minute of loading or waiting and before the 30-minute interruption that is required once more than 8 hours of driving have passed without one.

Before Assigning a Load: A Checklist

  • ☐ The hours plan fits the 11-hour, 14-hour and 30-minute rules, with dock time counted
  • ☐ The driver is qualified to drive the vehicle (CDL group and endorsements)
  • ☐ The weight and securement are within the limits (weight, securement)
  • ☐ Nobody is being pressed to drive tired or to break a limit (392.3, 390.6)
  • ☐ If the load came from a broker, the broker is registered and the paperwork names the right carrier (Load Boards)

Frequently Asked Questions

Is a dispatcher responsible if a driver breaks hours rules?

The carrier is: 395.3(a) bars a carrier from permitting or requiring a driver to drive beyond the limits, and 390.5 includes dispatching employees in "motor carrier". How liability is divided inside a company is outside this guide.

Does the coercion rule only cover carriers?

No. For the safety parts listed, it also names shippers, receivers and transportation intermediaries.

How long does a driver have to file a coercion complaint?

90 days after the event, in writing, under 386.12(c).
