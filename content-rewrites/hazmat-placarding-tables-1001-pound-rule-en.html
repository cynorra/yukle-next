Hazardous materials placarding is governed by one section of the federal rules, 49 CFR 172.504, and most mistakes come from misreading two things in it: which materials are in "Table 1" and "Table 2", and how the 1,001-pound threshold works. This guide sets out the logic in the order the rule applies it, with four hypothetical loads worked through. This is often shortened to hazmat placarding, and it is separate from the hazmat endorsement a driver needs on a CDL.

The Basic Rule

Under 172.504(a), each bulk packaging, freight container, unit load device, transport vehicle or rail car containing any quantity of a hazardous material must be placarded on each side and each end, with the placards specified in Tables 1 and 2 of that section, except as otherwise provided. The exceptions are what make the rule workable, and they differ between the two tables.

Class 6.2 (infectious substances) shows "NONE" in the table. Class 9 appears in Table 2, but 172.504(f)(9) says a CLASS 9 placard is not required for domestic transportation, including the part of international transportation that occurs within the United States. A bulk packaging must still carry its identification number on a CLASS 9 placard, an orange panel or a white square-on-point display.

The 1,001-Pound Exception

Under 172.504(c), when Table 2 materials move by highway or rail, placards are not required on a transport vehicle or freight container that contains less than 454 kg (1,001 pounds) aggregate gross weight of Table 2 materials. Three limits matter:

  • The exception applies only to Table 2 materials. Any amount of a Table 1 material brings the placard requirement back.
  • It does not apply to bulk packagings or to materials subject to 172.505.
  • The weight is the aggregate gross weight of all Table 2 materials on the vehicle, so quantities add up across categories rather than being judged one at a time.

The rule also says the exception does not prohibit displaying placards on vehicles that are not required to have them, unless another section prohibits it.

Four Loads, Placard or Not

These examples are illustrative, use non-bulk packages by highway, and assume nothing in 172.505 applies.

  1. 400 lb of a Class 3 flammable liquid and 500 lb of a Class 8 corrosive. Both are Table 2. The aggregate is 400 + 500 = 900 lb, which is under 1,001 lb, so the exception can apply and placards are not required.
  2. 400 lb of Class 3 and 700 lb of Class 8. The aggregate is 1,100 lb, which is 1,001 lb or more, so placards are required: FLAMMABLE and CORROSIVE, or the DANGEROUS placard if its conditions are met.
  3. 2,300 lb of Class 3 and 300 lb of Class 8, loaded at one facility. The DANGEROUS placard could normally replace separate placards for two categories, but 172.504(b) says that when 1,000 kg (2,205 lb) or more of one category is loaded at one loading facility, the placard for that category must be applied. 2,300 lb is above 2,205 lb, so FLAMMABLE must be displayed.
  4. 10 lb of a Division 1.1 explosive in a vehicle that otherwise holds 500 lb of Class 8. Division 1.1 is in Table 1, so it is placarded regardless of how little there is. The weight exception does not help.

Other Provisions Worth Knowing

  • DANGEROUS placard. A vehicle with non-bulk packages of two or more Table 2 categories needing different placards may display DANGEROUS instead of each separate placard, subject to the 1,000 kg rule above.
  • Multiple Class 1 divisions. When more than one division placard is required for Class 1 materials, only the lowest division number must be displayed.
  • COMBUSTIBLE and FLAMMABLE. A FLAMMABLE placard may be used instead of COMBUSTIBLE on a cargo tank or portable tank.
  • Empty packagings. Non-bulk packaging holding only residue of a Table 2 material need not be counted, except for materials subject to 172.505.
  • Poison placards. For domestic transportation, a POISON placard is not required on a vehicle already displaying POISON INHALATION HAZARD or POISON GAS.

Shipping papers, marking and labeling, placard design (49 CFR 172.519 to 172.560), bulk packaging rules and other modes of transport sit in other parts of the regulations. The hazardous materials endorsement a driver needs is covered in CDL Classes and Endorsements: What Each One Covers.
