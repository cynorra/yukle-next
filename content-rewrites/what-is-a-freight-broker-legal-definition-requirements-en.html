• A broker is defined by what it does, not by its title: a person other than a motor carrier or its employee or agent that sells, negotiates for or holds itself out as arranging transportation by motor carrier for compensation (49 U.S.C. 13102(2)).
  • A broker's registration stays in effect only while its $75,000 financial security does, and the statute lets a claimant reach that security for unpaid freight charges when the broker consents, ignores adequate notice, or a judgment has been entered (49 U.S.C. 13906(b)).
  • A registered broker must keep a record of each transaction for three years, may not work under another name or pose as a carrier, and may not give shippers, consignors or consignees anything of value beyond inexpensive advertising items (49 CFR 371.3, 371.7, 371.9).

"Freight broker" is a legal term as well as a job title. U.S. law defines who counts as one, requires registration and financial security, and sets conduct rules. The sections below give the definition, show how a broker differs from a carrier, a forwarder and a dispatcher, and set out the statutory requirements and the filings behind them. This guide gives no earnings figures and no application walkthrough because no primary source read for it supports either.

The Definition

Under 49 U.S.C. 13102(2), a broker is a person, other than a motor carrier or an employee or agent of a motor carrier, that as principal or agent sells, offers for sale, negotiates for, or holds itself out by solicitation, advertisement or otherwise as selling, providing or arranging for, transportation by motor carrier for compensation. 49 CFR 371.2 says a broker is a person who, for compensation, arranges or offers to arrange the transportation of property by an authorized motor carrier. Two features matter: a broker arranges transportation that a motor carrier performs, and holding itself out as arranging transportation can make a company a broker whatever it calls itself.

Broker, Carrier, Forwarder, Dispatcher: Three Cases

RoleWhat the law says
Motor carrierA person providing motor vehicle transportation for compensation (13102(14)). Moves the freight.
BrokerArranges transportation by a motor carrier for compensation and is not itself a motor carrier or an employee or agent of one (13102(2)).
Freight forwarderHolds itself out to the public to provide transportation of property for compensation and, in the ordinary course, assembles and consolidates shipments, assumes responsibility for the transportation from receipt to destination, and uses a carrier for part of it (13102(8)).
DispatcherNot a defined term in 13102. 49 CFR 390.5 includes employees responsible for dispatching drivers in "motor carrier".

An invented case for each. A company that books a trucking company for a shipper's load and keeps a fee is acting as a broker. A company that collects several shippers' freight into one trailer and answers to each shipper for the whole movement looks like a forwarder. A person who assigns loads to a carrier's drivers under that carrier's direction is a dispatcher, and 371.2 says an employee or bona fide agent of a carrier is not a broker when arranging shipments the carrier has accepted and bound itself to transport. The facts and the contract decide, not the label; see The Truck Dispatcher's Role.

Registration and Eligibility

  • Registration (13901, 13904(a)). A person may act as a broker for transportation subject to the Act only if registered. The Secretary registers a person who has sufficient experience and is fit, willing and able to be a broker and comply with the statute and regulations.
  • Duration (13904(b)). The registration remains in effect only while the broker is in compliance with 13906(b), the financial security rule.
  • Experience (13904(c)). Each broker must employ, as an officer, an individual with at least 3 years of relevant experience, or one who gives the Secretary satisfactory evidence of knowledge of the related rules, regulations and industry practices.
  • Carrier registration (13904(d)). A broker may not provide transportation as a motor carrier unless it has registered separately as one. The limit does not apply to a registered motor carrier or its employee or agent where the transportation is provided entirely by that carrier, with other registered motor carriers, or with rail or water carriers.
  • Updates (13904(g)). The registration must be updated within 30 days of a change in address, contact information, officers, process agent or other essential information.
  • Process agent (13304(a)). A broker must designate an agent in each State in which it operates on whom court process can be served (filed on Form BOC-3; 49 CFR part 366).

Financial Security

13906(b) allows registration as a broker only if the person files a surety bond, trust fund or other financial security that the Secretary finds adequate, and a trust fund or other security qualifies only if it consists of assets readily available to pay claims without resort to personal guarantees or collection of pledged accounts receivable. The security is available to pay a claim against the broker for failing to pay freight charges under its contracts in three situations: the broker consents; the broker does not respond to adequate notice and the surety provider determines the claim is valid; or the claim stays unresolved after a reasonable attempt and has been reduced to a judgment against the broker. A surety provider that receives notice of a claim must respond within 30 days and put any denial's grounds in writing. The amount is $75,000 regardless of the number of branch offices or sales agents (13906(b)(3); 49 CFR 387.307, effective 16 January 2026), and the Secretary must immediately suspend the registration of a broker whose available security falls below the required amount. The detail is in Freight Broker Bond and Trust Fund Rules.

Conduct Rules

  • Records (371.3). A record of each transaction for three years, showing the consignor, the originating carrier and its registration number, the bill of lading or freight bill number, the compensation received and who paid it, any non-brokerage service and its compensation, and any freight charges collected and the date paid to the carrier. Each party to the transaction may review it.
  • Name and status (371.7). No brokerage service in a name other than the one on the registration, and no representing operations as those of a carrier; advertising must show broker status.
  • Compensation (371.9). No compensation from a motor carrier where the broker owns or has a material beneficial interest in the shipment or controls it through common ownership with the shipper, and nothing of value to a shipper, consignor or consignee except inexpensive advertising items.

Where a Broker's Margin Comes From (Invented)

A shipper agrees to pay a broker $2,000. The broker pays the carrier $1,700. The broker's gross margin is $300, or 15 percent of the shipper's price ($300 ÷ $2,000). The broker's costs, and the carrier's costs, come out of those figures; no typical margin is stated because none is verified.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does a broker need its own trucks or a carrier registration?

No trucks are needed. A broker that also wants to provide transportation itself as a motor carrier must register separately as one (13904(d)).

Is a freight forwarder the same as a broker?

No. A forwarder assumes responsibility for the transportation from receipt to destination. A broker arranges.

Can a claimant collect from the bond?

For a broker's failure to pay freight charges under its contracts, in the three situations in 13906(b)(2)(A).

Scope: double brokering, cargo theft and State-level requirements are outside this guide, and none of this is legal advice.
