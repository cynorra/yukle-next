• NMFTA's published descriptions put parcel at up to about 150 lb per package, LTL at roughly 150 to 10,000 lb or one to six pallets, and full truckload at a load that fills a trailer, but these are typical ranges and each carrier sets its own cutoffs, some as low as 100 lb.
  • LTL trades cost for handling: the trailer carries several shippers' freight with more loading points, so transit can be longer and the chance of loss or damage higher than on a direct truckload.
  • Whether LTL or a full truckload costs less comes down to one break-even weight, the truckload price divided by the LTL rate per hundredweight, and every price in the example below is invented.

LTL, short for less-than-truckload, sits between parcel shipping and a full trailer. It is the usual way to move a few pallets without paying for a whole truck. This guide sets out what LTL is, how its typical size range compares with parcel and truckload, a break-even calculation, and what to confirm with a carrier. The size ranges come from NMFTA's public pages as shown in search excerpts; the pages themselves block automated access, so those figures are labelled secondary below and are not legal limits.

What LTL Is

In an LTL service the carrier builds each trailer from several shippers' freight, possibly with multiple pickup and drop-off stops, and each shipper pays for the space and handling its freight uses instead of the whole trailer. Because freight can be loaded and unloaded at more than one point, transit can take longer and the chance of loss or damage can be higher than when a load goes straight to its destination. That trade-off is why value and fragility matter when choosing LTL.

LTL or Truckload? A Break-Even (Invented Prices)

Suppose a carrier quotes LTL at $22 per hundredweight (100 lb) for a given lane and class, and a full truckload on the same lane at a flat $1,100. Both prices are invented.

  • Break-even weight = truckload price ÷ LTL rate per hundredweight = $1,100 ÷ $22 = 50 hundredweight, or 5,000 lb.
  • A 3,000 lb shipment: 30 × $22 = $660 by LTL, cheaper than $1,100.
  • A 6,000 lb shipment: 60 × $22 = $1,320 by LTL, more than $1,100 by truckload.

The comparison leaves out accessorial charges, minimum charges, transit time, and the higher handling risk of LTL, any of which can change the answer. Rates for real lanes are in the carrier's tariff or contract; none is stated here because none is verified.

What to Confirm Before Booking

  • Registration and insurance. A for-hire carrier must be registered (49 U.S.C. 13901), and the federal public-liability floor for non-hazardous property in interstate commerce is $750,000 for vehicles rated 10,001 lb or more (49 CFR 387.9); see the insurance guide. Check the carrier as in How to Check a Trucking Company.
  • Claims terms. A carrier may not allow less than 9 months to file a claim or less than 2 years to sue (49 U.S.C. 14706(e)). Ask for its liability limit and filing period in writing; any value limit must be declared or agreed (14706(c)(1)(A)). See Freight Claims.
  • Accessorials. Ask which fees apply for services such as liftgate or inside delivery and how reweigh and reclassification charges are handled; these come from the carrier's tariff or contract.
  • Paperwork. The carrier's bill of lading must show the number of packages, a description of the freight and, where it affects the rating, weight, volume or measurement (49 CFR 373.101), so put the correct count, dimensions and weight for each pallet on it; see Shipper, Carrier and Consignee.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is 150 lb a legal line between parcel and LTL?

No. It is a typical figure in NMFTA's descriptions. Carriers set their own cutoffs.

How many pallets can go LTL?

NMFTA describes one to six pallets as generally LTL, with the exact cutoff depending on the carrier.

Why can LTL cost more than expected?

Reclassification, reweighing and accessorial fees can change the total; confirm them with the carrier.
